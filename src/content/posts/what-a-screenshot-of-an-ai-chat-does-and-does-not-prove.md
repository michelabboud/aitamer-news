A screenshot is evidence of a display. The question is what claim that display is being asked to support.

The UNC Writing Center's guide to evidence says that evidence must support a particular claim. It discusses original documents and photographs as possible primary evidence while emphasizing that their connection to a claim still requires analysis. An image does not interpret itself, and neither does a screenshot of a conversation. I am applying that writing principle to a fictional chat artifact, not reporting a study of AI screenshots.

Here is the complete fictional description. A screenshot shows an assistant message saying, "The committee approved the proposal on Tuesday." The image includes the assistant's displayed text and the bottom edge of a conversation window. It does not show the user's earlier prompt, the conversation date, the model name, the settings, or messages above the crop. No alteration has been established, and no claim about who created the image is supplied.

The screenshot supports a narrow claim: those words appear in the displayed image. It does not establish what question produced them, which context the assistant received, whether the statement is factually correct, or whether the visible crop represents the whole exchange. It also does not establish when the message was generated or which system produced it.

An altered image is one possible explanation for a misleading screenshot, but the description gives no basis for alleging that this one was altered. A missing date or model label is an absence of context, not proof of fabrication. If the claim concerns what the assistant was asked, the uncropped conversation or a complete export could address more of that question. If the claim concerns whether the committee approved the proposal, the committee's own record is a more relevant artifact. A shareable conversation link, where available and appropriate, may add context, but context alone does not prove that the answer is correct.

My practical test is to write the claim before judging the screenshot. If the claim is "these words are visible," the image may support it. If the claim is "the assistant knew this fact," ask for the prompt, context, date, and source. If the claim is "the event happened," inspect an independent record of the event.

A screenshot is a window with a frame. The frame tells you what was displayed inside it. It does not automatically reveal what stood outside the crop.
