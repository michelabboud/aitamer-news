The revision requests I find least useful are the ones that ask only for polish: make it better, make it more concise, tighten it up. Those ask for polish, and polish is what they tend to get. The first draft comes back with smoother sentences and the same shape, and the writer who wanted a second draft has received a first draft with new clothes. That is my craft judgment, not a count of requests I have seen.

Here is the thing worth knowing: a second draft becomes genuinely different when the request changes one of the things that made the first draft what it was. Structure, audience, or assumption. Ask for a different one of those, and the draft has somewhere new to go. Ask only for quality, and it has nowhere to go except sideways.

Let me make the example myself, now. This is invented illustrative text, not something a particular model produced for me.

Suppose the first draft begins: "A library lends books to people who have a card." That is true and flat. Now watch what happens to it under three different requests.

Ask for a different audience, and the sentence becomes something aimed at a child: "A library is a big room full of books you can borrow. You take one home, read it, and bring it back so someone else can read it too."

Ask for a different assumption, and the sentence challenges the idea that a library is only for books: "A library is less a warehouse of books than a place people go to be around other people. The books are the excuse; the quiet company is the point."

Ask for a different structure, and the whole piece reorganizes, opening with a felt moment instead of a definition: "You remember the first book you checked out with your own card. This is about what happened after that."

Same subject. Three different pieces, because the requests named three different things.

There is a quiet agreement here with a much older source. The [UNC Writing Center's handout on audience](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/audience/) advises writers to keep their reader in mind because that is what helps them decide what material to include and how to organize and support the argument. That is advice for a person writing a paper, and it is not about AI. I am borrowing its point and applying it to revision requests: audience is one of the axes a second draft can move along, and naming it is what lets the request reach beyond polish.

The lesson is the request itself. Do not ask for a second draft. Ask for the draft that is structured as a story, or written for a beginner, or that assumes the opposite of the first draft. Name the axis you want moved, and the draft has somewhere to move.
