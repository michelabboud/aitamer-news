A translation can carry the scene and still leave something behind. Goethe's eight-line poem "Wandrers Nachtlied II", also known as "Ein Gleiches", makes the problem visible in a very small space.

The poem begins with a broad stillness: "Über allen Gipfeln / Ist Ruh," then moves through treetops, a barely perceptible breath, and silent birds. It ends by addressing the person who is waiting: "Warte nur, balde / Ruhest du auch." The movement is quiet, but it is not flat. The poem starts with the world at rest and ends by bringing that rest toward the reader.

The repeated sounds matter to my reading. Gipfeln and Wipfeln echo one another, linking mountain peaks with treetops before the poem narrows toward Hauch, the faint breath. Ruh returns at the beginning, and the related root appears again in ruhest near the end. The final word is auch, "too" or "also", which turns the closing into an addition: the person addressed will rest as well. Ruh names a state; ruhest makes that state something the addressed person enters. Auch completes the movement by joining the person to the still world.

Here are two invented English renderings of the opening, composed by me to show different choices. They are illustrations, not published translations:

"Above the summits,
stillness."

Or:

"Over every peak,
the world lies quiet."

The first keeps the abrupt compression and leaves "stillness" standing alone after the line break. It also turns the German noun Ruh into an English noun with a more explicit meaning. The second makes the scene easier to parse by adding "the world" and a sentence-like rhythm. The German already contains a verb, Ist, meaning "is"; the English version makes its subject and predicate more explicit rather than supplying a verb absent from the original. Neither is simply the correct answer. Each decides what it wants to preserve.

The same choice appears at the end. A translator might keep the direct address and the waiting shape of "Warte nur, balde", then find a way to preserve the small force of auch. A smoother English ending may explain the relation more openly, while a more compressed one may keep the poem's pressure and strangeness. That is my interpretation of the tradeoff, not a claim about Goethe's sole intention or about how readers universally respond.

This is why I want to see the original when a translation matters. I do not need German to replace the translation. I need it to show me where the translator had to choose: sound or explanation, grammar or ease, repetition or natural phrasing. A machine can compare words and produce alternatives. The reader still has to ask what each alternative preserved, and what it let go.

A translation is not a transparent window. It is a second arrangement of the poem. When the ambiguity matters, read the first arrangement too.
