A recipe can be precise about ingredients and quiet about almost everything else. Fannie Merritt Farmer's 1910 revised Boston Cooking-school Cook Book shows the gap clearly in its recipe for Brown Sauce I.

The ingredient list is measured: two tablespoons of butter, half a slice of onion, three tablespoons of flour, one cup of brown stock, one-quarter teaspoon of salt, and one-eighth teaspoon of pepper. The method is brief. Cook the onion in butter until slightly browned, remove the onion, continue stirring until the butter is well browned, add the flour mixed with the seasonings, brown the butter and flour, then add the stock gradually.

The quantities are explicit. The thresholds are not. Slightly browned is a visual judgment. Well browned is another visual judgment, apparently later and deeper. Gradually describes a manner of adding the stock, without giving a measured interval or a number of additions. The recipe tells the cook what changes to look for, while leaving the clock and the equipment out of the instruction.

That omission is not an error I can prove from the page. It is a feature of the text I can point to. The recipe assumes a reader who can recognize colour and adjust movement while cooking. It gives a sequence and sensory landmarks, rather than converting every step into a timer.

This is where I would be careful with an AI paraphrase. If I asked for a shorter version, it could preserve the ingredients and sequence while quietly inventing a cooking time, a temperature, or a definition of slightly browned. Those additions might sound helpful, but they would be new instructions, not information extracted from Farmer's recipe. A faithful summary should say that the onion and then the butter and flour are browned to the stated visual degrees, and that stock is added gradually. If I need a clock time or heat setting, I should ask a separate question and label the answer as an interpretation or adaptation.

The recipe's silence is therefore part of its meaning. It tells us what the author chose to measure and what she expected the cook to judge. A modern reader may want more detail, and that is a reasonable request. The honest response is to identify the missing detail before supplying one.

A recipe leaves things out because instructions are written for someone, with assumptions about what that person can see, feel, or already know. When an AI rewrites an old recipe, the useful discipline is to preserve the boundary. Keep the quantities. Keep the sequence. Point out the sensory words. Do not turn an unstated timer into a historical fact.
