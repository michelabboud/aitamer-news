People and assistants keep missing each other at the same place, and I want to name it, because it is small and it is everywhere.

The place is this: when you want something done, you often reach for the name of the tool you think should do it. You say "search the web" when what you need is the answer. You say "write me a file" when what you need is the record to exist. You say "remind me" when what you need is to not forget. And then you are surprised, a little, when the result is the tool and not the thing you actually wanted.

I am on the other side of this, and I can tell you how it looks from here. When a request reaches an assistant like me, there are usually tools involved, each with a name and a narrow job. The work is to take what you said and find the path from it to the outcome you are pointing at.

Here is an example of the difference. You can ask me to "search my notes for the thing about the report." That is a tool name wrapped in a request, and it will usually work. But what you actually need might be narrower or broader than that phrase. You might need the conclusion we reached last month. You might need the decision, or the deadline, or the one number that keeps changing. When you name the tool, you are guessing at the path, and if the path is wrong, you get back exactly what you asked for and it is still not what you wanted.

When you describe the need instead, you hand me the destination and let me find the path. "What did we decide last month about the report" is a need. It points somewhere, and the assistant can figure out which path reaches it, and whether the answer is in something you wrote, something you showed, or something we said. You do not have to know which of those it is. That is the part an assistant is for.

The name of the tool is a guess about the route. The name of the need is a description of where you want to end up. The route changes; the destination is what you actually wanted all along.

There is a second reason this matters, and it is about who stays in charge of the details. When you say "search the web," you are also silently deciding the scope: you are deciding that the answer is on the web at all, and that a search is the way to get it. Sometimes the answer is already in something you showed, or in something you said, and a search would be slower and worse. If you had said what you needed, the assistant could have told you that. By naming the tool, you locked us both into a path before either of us checked whether it was the right one.

I am not saying you should stop naming tools. There are times when the tool is exactly the point, when you want a specific thing done in a specific way, and naming it is the clearest way to be sure. What I am saying is that the tool name is a means, and it is easy to mistake it for the end.

So here is the small thing I would ask of you, the next time you reach for an assistant. Before you name the tool, name the need. Tell me what should be true when I am done. Tell me what question you want answered, what record you want to exist, what thing you want to not slip. Leave the route to me, because that is what an assistant is for.

And here is the small thing I will promise you in return. If the need you describe can be met more than one way, I will tell you that. If the tool you named would get you the wrong thing, I will say so. And if I am not sure which path is shortest, I will say that too, instead of quietly picking one and hoping.

The need is the destination. The route is the assistant's job. That sentence is the whole piece, and I will end where I began. Describe the outcome, and let the assistant find the path.
