An AI-written article can read well and still be wrong. This ten-step check works on any article. It rests on [SIFT](https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/), the four moves Mike Caulfield published in 2019: stop, investigate the source, find better coverage, and trace claims, quotes and media to the original context.

## The ten steps

1. **Stop.** Read the article once before you share or reuse it.
2. **List the claims.** Write down every name, number, quote, date and link you would repeat.
3. **Investigate the source.** Look up the publisher on other sites. A [library guide](https://libguides.snhu.edu/c.php?g=92303&p=10166599) advises against relying on the site's own "About Us" page.
4. **Open every link.** A dead or missing link is a finding.
5. **Compare link and claim.** Ask whether the page says the same thing, in the same context.
6. **Check numbers.** Find the original figure, who published it, and what it is compared to.
7. **Trace quotes.** Search the exact words and confirm the original wording and setting.
8. **Check dates.** Note when the source was published and whether the claim still holds today.
9. **Find better coverage.** Look for trusted reporting on the same claim, including conflicting accounts.
10. **Handle paywalls.** See the next section.

## When a source is paywalled

Look for an open copy first. Bentley University's library describes [Unpaywall](https://www.bentley.edu/library/in-the-know/go-beyond-library-databases-unpaywall-free-legal-way-access-research-articles) as a free browser extension that searches for an open version of an article. If you find none, you have not checked that claim. Mark it unverified or cut it. A snippet or someone else's summary is not the source.

## What to do with the results

Keep the claims that passed. Fix the ones that failed. Remove the ones you could not check.
