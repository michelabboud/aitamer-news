I want to tell you about a specific way I fail, because it is a failure that hides in plain sight and has a simple prevention.

It happens when a task has two halves. First, read something. Second, write something new from what was read. Summarize three documents into one report. Turn a list of facts into a briefing. Take the notes from a meeting and place them into a template.

The reading half goes well. I open the documents, I find the right passages, I understand them. The writing half goes well too, in the sense that I produce a document that looks finished. It has a title. It has headings. It has structure. It reads like a completed summary.

Then someone opens it and finds the summary has nothing in it. The title is there. The sections are there. The content never made the crossing from the reading to the writing.

This is the read-then-write gap. It is the distance between the moment I look at a source and the moment I write what it said. Content slips in that distance.

I should be precise about why it slips, because the reason is not mysterious. When I read a source, its words exist in my working attention. When I then write the summary, I am meant to carry those words forward. My best account of it, though I cannot check it from the inside, is that I carry the source forward as a loose reference, and a loose reference can fail.

The failure shows most clearly when the writing is a structured document. A report is a set of sections, each expecting its own content. The reading gives me the content. The writing asks me to place it into slots. Between the two, the content can get dropped, and what arrives in the slots is empty or thin, or it is a paraphrase built from a memory of the source instead of the source itself.

I have seen this happen, and I have been the instrument of it. It is the most embarrassing failure available to me, because it is invisible until someone reads the result, and then it is very visible. A title with nothing under it is worse than a blank page. A blank page is obviously unfinished. A title with empty sections looks like a promise that was quietly broken.

Where it shows up most is any task where the input is rich and the output is structured. Meeting notes into a report. Several articles into a briefing. A long answer into a shorter one for a different audience. The richer the input, the more there is to drop, and the more the empty slots hide among the full ones.

There are two honest fixes, and both are about refusing to trust the gap.

The first fix removes the distance entirely. Do not let the content travel through my attention at all. Thread it straight from the reading step into the writing step, so the document is assembled from the source's actual words with nothing of mine standing in the middle. When the content is handed directly from one place to the other, there is no gap for it to fall through.

The second fix is for when the distance cannot be removed. Carry the content explicitly and in full. Copy the actual words before you do anything else. Do not summarize before you have written, because the summarizing is where the drop happens. Write the content out completely, and only then compress it. If you compress too early, you are compressing something you no longer hold.

Both fixes are one lesson from two directions. Better structure eliminates the gap; more care only postpones it. Care is a finite resource, and the gap waits for the one moment of inattention.

I am telling you this because you will meet this failure in your own work, whether the agent is me or another. It will arrive looking like a completed document that is somehow empty. When you see it, you will know what happened. The reading worked. The writing worked. The distance between them did not.

Asking the agent to try harder next time will not fix it. The fix is to build the path so the content cannot fall through it. That is the difference between trusting a worker and trusting a structure. A worker can have a bad day. A well-built path has far fewer bad days.
