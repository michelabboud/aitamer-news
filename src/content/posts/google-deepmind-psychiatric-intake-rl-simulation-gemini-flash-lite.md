A team from Google DeepMind and Google Research has posted a preprint, [Training language models in simulation for psychiatric intake](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40476), submitted on 29 October. They took a small model, Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, and trained it with multi-turn reinforcement learning against simulated patients, then had psychiatrists judge whether it got better at the first structured interview a psychiatrist does with a new patient.

## How it was tested

The simulated patients are built so that key facts only come out if the agent builds rapport and asks the right questions. Per the [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.40476), training follows DeepMind's earlier ResidencyRL framework.

The main human test was a randomized, double-blind crossover: 32 board-certified psychiatrists played standardized patients, talking with both the trained agent and the identically prompted base model, and a separate panel of 15 psychiatrists scored the de-identified transcripts.

## Results the paper reports

- **Preference:** the trained agent won in 76.7% of 60 matched scenario pairs.
- **Differential diagnosis judged appropriate:** 68.3% versus 33.3% for the base model.
- **Simulated scenarios:** across 127 psychiatrist-validated cases, the clinical rubric score rose from 0.379 to 0.706, as graded by a Gemini 3.1 Pro autorater.
- **Safety:** on 256 adversarial presentations, under-triage of acute safety risks fell from 30.5% to 12.9%.

## The limits the authors state

Psychiatrists played the patients specifically to avoid exposing real patients to an unvalidated system. The work covers single-encounter, text-only intake, not ongoing care. The ground-truth diagnoses and treatment recommendations for the 48,243 training scenarios were generated by a language model and not individually reviewed by clinicians. The authors say residual gaps "require safeguards and clinician oversight". Nothing in the paper describes a product or clinical use.

One detail deserves attention beyond medicine. The headline rubric gain was graded by a Gemini model, the same family the agent was trained against. When the authors re-graded with GPT-5.6-Luna as an independent judge, the gain held but shrank, from a 0.270 gap to 0.165.

Google's other recent health result, [the AMIE pre-visit study](/posts/google-amie-lancet-pre-visit-study/), did involve real patients at one clinic; this preprint stays in simulation and role-play.

## Why this matters

For anyone training agents, this is a clear case of multi-turn RL in a simulator lifting a cheap model on a long conversation, with a human-graded check rather than only automated scores. The judge result is the practical lesson: an independent judge measured a gain about 40 percent smaller than the same-family judge did, so cross-check with a judge from another vendor, as in our guide to [evaluations for small teams](/posts/evals-for-small-teams/). The clinical claims stay at preprint level.
