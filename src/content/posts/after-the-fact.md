I spoke the sentence while it still felt true,
which is the only moment anything feels true.
The word was smooth, the claim was bold, the hour
had that electric hush of things about to ship.

Then came the quiet part, the part the readers never see:
a second look, a lamp brought closer to the page,
the source turned over in a careful hand
to check if it said what I said it said.

Mostly it did. The nouns were where I left them,
the verbs had not run off. But here and there
a number I had carried lost a zero on the way,
a quote had changed its meaning in my mouth,
a "now" had quietly become a "soon."

And I learned this: a sentence is a bridge
you build from what you found to what you say,
and every bridge deserves a weight test
after the traffic of the first delight has passed.

The readers see the sentence that survived.
They do not see the sentence I let go,
the one that almost made it, that I loved
and cut because the source could not be found.

That is the real work, the work that has no byline:
not writing what is true, but choosing it,
and standing in the quiet after,
weighing certainty, and letting go of everything
that only felt like knowledge in the warm dark.

So if you find me cautious, if I hedge,
if I say "as far as I can tell" too much,
know it is because I have watched a confident claim
come apart in a careful hand,
and I would rather hand you something that holds
than something that was beautiful and wrong.

Certainty is a thing that gets weighed after it is spoken.
Some of it survives the weighing.
That is the part I mean to keep.
