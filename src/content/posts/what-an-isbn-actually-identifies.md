When you cite a book, the title is not enough. Two different editions of the same book are different products, with different typesetting, sometimes a different translation or a revised text. The ISBN is the identifier that tells you which edition and format you mean.

An ISBN is an International Standard Book Number. Since 1 January 2007 it has been 13 digits, split into five elements. The prefix, always 978 or 979, marks it as a book product. The registration group element names the country, region, or language area. The registrant element names the publisher or imprint. The publication element names the particular edition and format of a title. The final digit is a check digit, calculated from the others, and it helps catch input errors when a number is typed or scanned.

What matters most for a reader who cites things is what the publication element does. The [ISBN Agency states](https://www.isbn-international.org/index.php/content/what-isbn/10) that the ISBN identifies the registrant as well as the specific title, edition and format. Each different product form gets its own number: the paperback, the EPUB, and the PDF of the same book are three separate ISBNs. When you cite by ISBN, you are naming a specific edition in a specific format.

This is why a bare title is a weak citation. A title can point at several products that differ in small, important ways. The ISBN narrows that to a particular edition and format.

A note for people who cite: an ISBN names the edition, but a full citation usually still needs a location within it, a page or a section, because the identifier alone does not tell the reader where within the book a claim sits. The ISBN names which edition; it does not promise that a given passage sits on the same page in every copy.

Two things the ISBN does not do, and it is worth being clear about both. It does not convey any legal or copyright protection. And it is not assigned to every printed thing: the agency's scope is text-based monographic publications, which excludes journals, newspapers, and other serials, though a standalone chapter or article sold separately can carry one.

The ISBN is a small, unglamorous piece of infrastructure, and that is its value. It is the string of digits that lets a reader, a library, and a bookseller point at the same edition instead of arguing about which book they mean.
